# Analogies

How to find an analogy that explains, not one that decorates. Use it at the concept gate (Phase 3 in `SKILL.md`), before any sketch.

A weak analogy shares the topic's look: a coin for payments, a brain for AI, a rocket for growth. A strong one shares how the thing works: its parts, what pushes on what, where it breaks. The audience already understands the second thing, so they borrow that understanding for yours.

## When to use one

- Use one when the mechanism is unfamiliar to this audience and can't be shown directly.
- Skip it when the real thing can be shown: the actual form, screen, chart or photograph. The real object beats a metaphor for it.
- Skip it when a number with its comparison already makes the point.

## The method

1. **Say the mechanism in one plain sentence, with verbs.** Not "the team ensures compliance" but "every item stops with us, we check it against a list, and only clean ones go through." If you can't write the sentence, the analogy will be vague too. Settle the slide's job first.
2. **List the relations.** Drop the nouns and keep the structure: what flows, what blocks, what is checked, what is traded for what, what is hidden, what builds up, what fails when one part fails. Mark the one relation this slide is about.
3. **Generate widely.** Write 8 to 12 candidates from different domains, starting from the audience's own world: their commute, kitchen, sport, job, city. The first two or three ideas are usually the stock ones, so keep going past them.
4. **Map each candidate part by part.** Concept part on one side, analogy part on the other. If the relation the slide is about has no counterpart, drop the candidate.
5. **Find where it breaks.** Every analogy breaks somewhere. That's fine when the break is away from the point. It's fatal when the audience would draw a wrong conclusion about the point itself.
6. **Score the survivors**, then offer 2 or 3 with their mapping and break point, and recommend one.

## Scoring

| Test | Passes when |
|---|---|
| Stranger test | Someone with no background gets it from the picture, with no caption. |
| Mapping holds | The relation the slide is about maps cleanly. |
| Safe break | Where it breaks doesn't mislead about the point. |
| Drawable | It is a recognisable object or scene, and the medium can render its tells (concept gate, check 5). |
| Can perform | It can move to do the claim: the barrier lifts, the blur resolves, the water line drops. |
| Not stock | Not the first image anyone would think of, and not on the stock list in `references/anti-slop.md`. |
| Fits the room | This audience knows the source thing. Check culture, age and field: a sport they don't follow fails. |

## Worked examples

**The team explainer** took five rounds in the source deck (`references/case-studies.md`). Scored after the fact, the method would have settled it in one conversation.

Mechanism: items move in both directions; each one stops with the team, is checked, and is either released or held.

| Candidate | What it maps | Where it breaks | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control tower | Coordination | Towers direct traffic; they don't stop and inspect each item | Wrong relation |
| Conveyor | Steady flow | No check, no hold | Wrong relation |
| Routing hub of moving dots | Flow | No object to recognise | Not drawable as a thing |
| Checkpoint | Stop and check | One direction only | Partial |
| Two-way border post with a guard and a boom barrier | Stop, inspect, release or hold, both ways | Border posts check people, not this content | Accepted. The break is away from the point. |

**Shutter speed** was approved on sight. Mechanism: working fast costs accuracy; slowing down makes the result clean. Mapping: speed is motion, errors are blur, slowing down is the image resolving. It also performs: the smears slow, sharpen and settle into a clean row, so the slide does the trade instead of stating it.

## Where to look

Prompts for step 3, grouped by the relation. They are starting points; the right source usually comes from this audience's world, not this list.

| The concept | Look for things that | To start from |
|---|---|---|
| Checks or filters | stop each item and inspect it | border post, ticket barrier, flour sieve, airport tray |
| Trades one thing for another | have a dial with two ends | shutter speed, heat against cooking time, bicycle gears |
| Hides most of itself | are mostly out of sight | iceberg, roots, plumbing behind a wall, a kitchen behind the pass |
| Builds up, then fails | accumulate quietly | limescale in a kettle, a queue at a lift, sand in an hourglass |
| Depends on its weakest part | fail at one point | a blown fuse, a zip with one bad tooth, a dropped baton |
| Hands work along | pass something in sequence | a restaurant pass, a relay, a sorting office |
| Moves a lot from a little | have leverage | a rudder, a valve, railway points |
| Must stay in balance | have two sides that must match | a scale, a seesaw, a ledger |

## Carrying it through

- Draw it fully. The iceberg needs a bottom and real water. Half a metaphor reads worse than none.
- Draw only the parts that map. Extra detail invites the audience to map things that don't.
- One analogy per idea, and never two on one slide.
- A governing analogy can return later in the deck as an echo. Don't force every slide into it.
- Don't label it ("Think of it as a border post"). The picture says it. The speaker can say the bridge once, in their own words.
- If it breaks somewhere that matters, put the limit in the notes so the speaker can name it.

## Signs it's the analogy, not the drawing

- It needs a caption to make sense.
- The user asks whether it's the right analogy. Go back to step 1, not to the art.
- Different drawings of it keep getting the same complaint. Change the analogy.
- The reverse: the same analogy keeps getting complaints about how it looks ("doesn't look like a planet"). That is the medium. See the planet in `references/case-studies.md`.
